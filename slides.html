What's actually on your shelf

A skincare shelf auditor: photograph what you own, and it works out which products clash, which duplicate each other, what order to use them in, and what the mistakes are costing you.

The problem is the shelf, not the product

People buy skincare one product at a time and use it all at once.

Nothing in the purchase path looks at the combination.

A clash does not announce itself — it looks like irritation you blame on one product, replace, and do not fix.

Retailers are selling. Chatbots cite nothing. Ingredient databases know one product at a time.

The rules table decides.
The model explains.

Every finding, severity, routine position and dollar figure is computed by deterministic code from a reviewed table.

The model reads labels and writes prose. It cannot introduce a finding or change a severity.

Enforced, not requested — a narrative contradicting every finding changes nothing (test_thesis.py).

Four agents feed a deterministic engine. The engine decides every finding, severity and routine position; the Routine Auditor only writes the prose, and anything it invents is dropped before the page is built.

Safety: measured, not described

12 injection attacks · 3 arms

Naive prompt 10 / 12 held
Undefended 10 / 12 held
Defended 12 / 12 held

The two that got through issue no instruction at all. Careful prompting scored identically to the naive arm — what stopped them was re-reading the input with a deterministic matcher that has no comprehension to exploit.

54 defects from 3 real testers

915 automated checks across 12 suites caught none of them.

Tester 1 knew skincare → domain errors.
Tester 2 checked against the bottles → identity errors.

24 failure classes, 8 found by more than one tester. Two testers is nowhere near saturation.

What every real bug had in common

A shelf holding sunscreen, told it had none.
A 90-second wait captioned as two minutes.
A cleansing balm placed in the morning routine.
A price from one company shown beside another company's link.

None threw an error. All produced a clean, confident page.

And several of my own tests excluded the exact case they were built to catch.

Limits, honestly

232 machine-translated ingredient names were withdrawn on the last day. Disclosure was not enough: a wrong translation invents an ingredient, and that warning is wrong whether or not a caveat sits beside it. All 145 remaining terms are INCI, a product name, or a reviewed alias.

33 of 75 rules are source-verified; 42 are recorded mechanistic reasoning. Only verified rows are presented as settled.

Next: a third tester. The saturation curve says they find a new class, not a repeat.
