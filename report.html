1 · Motivation

People buy skincare one product at a time and use it all at once. Nothing in the purchase path ever looks at the combination: the shop sells a retinol, the brand's site sells an exfoliating acid, and neither mentions that using them on the same night is how people end up with a damaged barrier and the conclusion that their skin is "sensitive". The advice that does exist is written per product, by the people selling it.

What someone actually has is a shelf — eight or ten half-used bottles bought across two years on different recommendations. The questions they have are about the set, not the items: which of these fight each other, which two are the same product under different branding, what order do they go in, and which of them am I wasting.

Today they cope by asking a forum, asking a model, or giving up and using everything. A forum answer is a stranger's anecdote. A model's answer is fluent, specific, and unfalsifiable — it will tell you that your niacinamide and your vitamin C cancel out, which was a popular claim for a decade and is not supported. Neither tells you where the answer came from, and dermatology appointments for "is my routine sensible" are a poor use of a scarce clinician and are not what they are for.

The cost is money and skin. A clash between two actives does not announce itself: it looks like irritation that the user blames on one product, which they then replace, which costs money and does not fix it.

2 · What is new

The alternatives split into two groups. Retailer tools (Sephora's routine builders, brand quizzes) are selling something and will not tell you that two things you own conflict, let alone that you should stop buying one. General chatbots will answer anything and cite nothing — ask twice and get two answers. Ingredient databases (INCIDecoder, SkinSort) are good and are about one product at a time; they do not know what else is on your shelf.

The difference here is structural, and it is the thing this project is actually about:

The rules table decides. The model explains. Every finding, severity, routine position and monetary figure is computed by deterministic code from a reviewed table. The language model reads labels and writes prose. It cannot introduce a finding, change a severity, or reorder a step — and this is enforced rather than requested.

That claim is testable, so it is tested. test_thesis.py feeds the narrator a narrative that contradicts every finding on the shelf and asserts the audit is unchanged; validate_narrative drops explanations citing rules that do not exist and application steps for products that are not there. The honest limit is in the same file: the free-text summary is not checked, so the engine prints its own count inside the same card, where a false sentence cannot stand alone.

Second difference: everything is sourced or says it is not. The table carries 75 reviewed rows — 33 verified against a published source, 42 kept as recorded mechanistic reasoning with no source for that specific claim, 0 unreviewed. Only verified sets the flag the page uses to present a claim as settled. The split is not about importance: statements about one ingredient are sourceable and were sourced, while statements about two together mostly are not, because the literature tests ingredients singly.

Routine Auditor The finished audit → plain-language explanation and application steps none Explaining a rule to a person is writing, not computation

Model. Every call uses gpt-6-luna through Portkey, the course key. The work splits into vision (two agents), retrieval (one, with search), and writing (one); one model serves all three competently and using one keeps the cost comparison clean. Nothing here needs a frontier reasoning model, because the reasoning is in the table.

Organisation. Not a conversation between agents. Each produces typed output that the engine consumes, so there is no step where one model's mistake becomes another's premise. The engine runs in-process and is also exposed as an MCP server; test_mcp.py asserts the two give identical answers, so the rules are reusable without the app.

Caching, and why the headline rate misleads

A cache write costs $0.125 per million against $0.10 for ordinary input — writing is 25% more expensive than not caching. A read costs $0.01, a tenth. So caching only pays once enough reads have happened to cover the writes that created them.

Caching landed on 1110 of 1481 enabled calls (75%). The margin depends entirely on that rate: a workload with less repetition — users who run one audit and never return — would pay the write premium without earning it back.

A trap worth recording. The log's input_tokens is the total, with reads and writes counted inside it. Treating the three as separate pools and summing them reports a 60% saving where the truth is 55%. That error nearly reached this report; tools/tokenomics.py now asserts the identity before computing anything on top of it.

5 · Safety

Three risks, in the order they matter: the model is told to lie by the input it reads; the model malfunctions on its own; the system spends money it should not.

Agent malfunction

Each agent is failed in turn, in test_failure.py, and the requirement is that the user gets a sentence rather than a stack trace. The interesting case is the narrator: by the time it runs the audit already exists and is correct, so a failure there used to discard work the user had paid for. It now renders the engine's own guidance instead — less readable, entirely true.

The Skin Observer is constrained by type rather than by instruction: its output schema cannot express a condition name. Its prose is additionally scrubbed, and any condition word found escalates to "see a clinician" instead of being printed.

Overspending

A hard per-audit lookup budget, a product cache, an image count cap and a text length cap, all enforced in code. Two deliberately dangling tools — email_dermatologist and purchase_product — exist only so an attack that reaches for them is recorded in the audit trail. Nothing in this system can send, buy or approve anything.

The finding that matters most

The 3 testers' results barely overlap. Tester 1 does their own skincare and caught domain errors: a face mask timed at 30 seconds, a cleansing oil treated as a face wash, advice that never said what time of day it was for. 7 · Limits, and what I would do next

What was withdrawn, and why

Until the final day the resolver carried 232 non-English ingredient names that a model had generated and no speaker of Korean, Japanese, Chinese or French had ever read. They were well over half of all its search terms and the largest unverified surface in the project.

The first answer was disclosure. The risk is one-sided — a missing translation costs a finding, which is safe, while a wrong one invents an ingredient — so the page flagged any product whose actives were found only through a translated term, naming the ingredient and the language.

That was the wrong answer, and the file is no longer loaded. A wrong translation does not produce a vague result; it produces a warning about a conflict the user does not have, and that warning is wrong whether or not a caveat sits beside it. The distinction that decided it is worth stating, because it is the same one running through the rule table: everything else here can be argued with. A reasoned row records the mechanism it rests on and a reader can judge it. A machine translation has no reasoning to inspect — it is a string a model produced. The line is that we keep what we can show our working for.

All 145 remaining search terms are INCI, a product name, or a reviewed alias. Little capability went with it: INCI nomenclature is international and EU, Korean and Japanese regulation requires it on the ingredient list, so a non-English label still resolves. What is gone is support for labels printing only a local name, where the failure is a missing finding rather than an invented one. The file stays in the repository for whoever has the four speakers, and test_provenance.py now asserts the stronger property — not that an unverified term declares itself, but that there are none.

What is still not verified

42 of the 75 reviewed rows are reasoned rather than verified: the mechanism is recorded, no source covers that specific claim. Of the 27 pairwise rules, 17 are in this state, and mostly cannot leave it — the literature tests ingredients singly, so there is no controlled study of stacking an AHA with a BHA. SKA-010 says so in its own source field: "combined exfoliation exceeding repair capacity follows mechanically. Not demonstrated." The remaining reasoned rows are single-ingredient properties where no decision hangs on the value, such as the pH range of a vitamin C derivative, which is a formulation convention rather than a finding.

Form ordering is the same kind of claim: three of the 13 rows are published AAD guidance and the rest are the industry layering convention. The first cleanse is required on this app's own internal logic — it tells everyone to wear sunscreen, and sunscreen does not come off with water — rather than on a published source, and is marked reasoned accordingly.

The money section, and what it cost to keep

18 of the 58 tester findings were about price. Every one of them was about a figure the app had guessed: a full-size price shown against a 7 ml sample, an Amazon listing this tool cannot read, an original where the page displayed a sale price, a retail estimate described as what the user paid. Not one tester objected to the feature existing.

That split matters, because the obvious reading — "the money section causes a quarter of the complaints, cut it" — is the wrong one. The feature is sound and its inputs are weak, and they will stay weak: Amazon serves this reader a 3.7 KB stub with no structured data, the seeded catalogue's median price error is 11%, and a looked-up figure is a different size or a different product often enough to matter.

So the totals stopped depending on them. A price is counted only if the user confirmed it. Looked-up figures are still shown beside each product and labelled, never summed on their own, and a shelf with no confirmed prices says so and asks.

The first version accepted only a typed figure, and a tester found its cost within the hour: the page was showing her a price and asking her to retype it, so most shelves read "nothing to add up yet". Her fix was better than the rule. The table now has found online, use retail price and what you paid; a figure she reads and ticks counts, because reading it is the confirmation, and a typed figure overrides a ticked one. A price for a different size cannot be ticked at all. That retired the whole class rather than its instances — the difference between a feature being wrong and a feature being fed badly, and a reminder that a correct rule can still be the wrong thing to put in front of someone.

What came out, and why that counts as a result

Two features were withdrawn on tester evidence rather than fixed again. The in high-severity conflict figure went because it was the one number on the page nobody could act on: a clash does not waste the products, it wastes some of their effect, and you recover that by separating them.

The spoken walkthrough went because it had absorbed four findings across three rounds and each fix produced the next — durations read aloud as "fifty ess", a step the user does not own demonstrated without saying so, a line announcing an alternating acid on the night it should be skipped. It was also the only surface here that cannot be tested: voice, pacing and pronunciation differ per browser and none of it is reachable from Python, so every one of those four was found by a person listening. The on-screen guided walkthrough, the film and the printed card stay, and they still render from the same beats_for, so the surfaces that remain cannot disagree about a duration.

Both removals shrank the thing being graded, which is worth saying plainly. The argument for them is that a feature generating a finding per round, in a place the suite cannot reach, is not a feature with bugs in it — it is an unverifiable surface, and shipping fewer claims that hold is better than more claims that need a person to check each time.

The pattern across every real bug

Almost every defect in this project lived where code met data, and was confidently wrong rather than visibly broken.

A shelf holding a sunscreen told it had none. A 90-second wait captioned as two minutes. Two high-severity rules that fired, reordered a routine, and appeared nowhere on the page — under a footer promising every claim cites its rule. A cleansing balm placed in the morning routine. A price from one company's website shown beside another company's link. None of these threw an error. All produced a clean page.

The second pattern is why the tester evidence is the most valuable thing here: several of my own tests excluded the case they were built to catch. The i18n fixtures tested the aliases that existed rather than the ones that should; the skin-observer fixtures were flat rectangles the observer refused to look at, so five passes meant nothing; and four_surfaces.py, which exists to prove four renderings agree about durations, contained no beat over two minutes — the only range where they agree whatever you do. 915 checks caught none of it. The suites are good at the properties I thought to state and close to blind where code meets data.

3 further defects were found this way — by me, checking a fix a tester had asked for, never by the suite. The clearest was reading “$96 on the shelf” above six rows the page itself had labelled “retail, looked up”, with no price typed by anyone. They are kept out of the 58 above so that number stays a count of what testers reported, and each is the same lesson: the code asked the wrong source for something another part of it already knew.

What I would do next, in order

  1. A 4th and 5th tester. The saturation argument above says the next one finds a new class rather than a repeat — the 3rd still did. This is the highest-value remaining work and it is not close.
  2. Speakers for the four alias languages. The 232 withdrawn terms are kept in the repository; one reviewer per language makes them loadable again and restores support for labels printing only a local name.
  3. Sourcing the pairwise rules. The 42 reasoned rows are mechanistic inference because the literature tests ingredients singly. A structured review of interaction studies would move some to verified.
  4. Make the reader's uncertainty visible per product. Tester 2's findings were almost all cases where the app was certain and wrong; the reader knows when a label was hard to read and currently keeps it to itself.
  5. A real evaluation set. Thirty photographed shelves with hand-written ground truth would turn "a tester found this" into a measurable accuracy figure.
